When should you start studying for the MCAT?

Most advice says to wait until junior year, and that's fair for dedicated prep — you shouldn't grind full-length practice exams as a freshman. But the science the MCAT tests is sitting in your classes right now. General Chemistry, Biology, Organic Chemistry, Physics, Biochemistry, Psychology and Sociology are all MCAT content. If you learn that material a little more deeply the first time you see it, your junior-year prep becomes review instead of a panic. The best time to start isn't "junior year" — it's the moment you're covering the material in class.

MCAT prep in freshman and sophomore year

Waypoint maps each course you take onto the official MCAT content areas and their exam weighting, so you can see honestly how much of the test your coursework already covers. Finish Biology I and you've touched cell biology, genetics, and molecular biology. Add General Chemistry and Organic Chemistry and your coverage climbs. It shows you how the MCAT will bend what your professor teaches — the exam tests the consequence, not just the definition — and lets you practice that gap a few minutes at a time, on the exact topics you're studying now.

Premed prerequisites and MCAT subjects

Most medical schools require a year each of Biology, General Chemistry, Organic Chemistry, and Physics (all with labs), plus English and a math or statistics course. For the MCAT itself, finish those plus Biochemistry before you sit the exam — it's heavily tested. Psychology and Sociology are also on the MCAT (about 18% of it together), though they usually aren't admission requirements.

  • Required by most med schools: Biology I & II, General Chemistry I & II, Organic Chemistry I & II, Physics I & II, English, math/statistics
  • Strongly recommended before the MCAT: Biochemistry
  • MCAT-tested content: everything above, plus Psychology, Sociology, and CARS (critical reading)
